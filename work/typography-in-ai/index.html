My final degree project in Graphic Design studied how two image generators, Midjourney and DALL·E 3, handle the design of letters. I wrote one prompt for each letter of the Spanish alphabet, ran it in both tools and compared the results letter by letter.

The theory. The first half of the memoir covers the history of typography and its move to digital, type anatomy, classification and legibility, the software used to draw type today, and the history of AI, from the first neural networks to current image generators.

The experiment. Each of the 27 letters, from A to Z with the Ñ, got its own style: an A in the manner of William Morris, a D built from Japanese imperial architecture, an O shaped as a half-full whisky bottle. Every prompt produced four images per tool, 216 in total.

Every letter was analysed on four points: whether the shape is precise and readable, how the ornament fits the letterform, how faithfully the tool read the prompt, and the level of detail and finish. Below, the William Morris A, first from Midjourney and then from DALL·E.

Midjourney. It kept the letters clearer and more consistent, worked the ornament into the shape and followed the style of each prompt more closely.

DALL·E. It was more experimental and less predictable. The ornament often sat on top of the letter instead of being part of it, and some letters came out with deformed anatomy.

What went wrong. Letters came out with extra arms or strokes, stretched parts or as a different letter altogether, and heavy textures sometimes buried the shape. Writing the prompt between quotation marks made both tools paint text into the background, so many prompts had to be rewritten with more detail.

The Ñ. Neither tool could draw it. Midjourney returned coral shapes with no letter in them, and DALL·E returned other letters made of coral. The first two images below are from Midjourney, the last two from DALL·E.

I laid out the memoir in InDesign for print, with one spread of analysis per letter, and a 21 × 21 cm square book where each spread shows one letter, with Midjourney on the left page and DALL·E on the right.

The project concluded that these tools can make striking letters but not a coherent typeface, and that every result still needs a designer to correct it outside the tool. It rejected the idea that AI can design type on its own and pointed to working together, AI and designer, as the most promising route.
